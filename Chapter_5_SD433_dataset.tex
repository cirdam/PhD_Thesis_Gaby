The measurement of the cosmic-ray energy spectrum poses several experimental challenges.
Since this observable carries important astrophysical information, the construction of a clean and well-understood dataset is essential.
This requires not only a reliable event reconstruction, but also a careful characterization of the detector response, the trigger hierarchy, and the stability of the array over time.

This chapter introduces the main elements that define the dataset used in this work.
It begins with the hierarchical trigger chain, from the local station triggers to the selection of physical events, described in \cref{sec:triggers}.
\cref{sec:efficiency} addresses the trigger efficiency and establishes the energy threshold above which the exposure can be treated as purely geometrical.
The discussion closes with the stability and operational history of the array in \cref{sec:stability}, which provides the basis for the exposure calculation in \cref{sec:efficiency} used in the spectrum measurement.

\section{The Surface Detector Triggers}
\label{sec:triggers}

To discriminate signals produced by an \gls{eas}, initiated by the interaction of a primary \gls{cr} in the atmosphere, the surface detector uses a hierarchical trigger system.
This hierarchy begins at the level of individual stations and continues up to the selection of physical events and quality requirements at the array level.
A schematic summary of the trigger chain is shown in \cref{fig:t1t2t3}.

Originally, two T1 trigger modes were implemented at the station level: \gls{th}-T1, and \gls{tot}.
The \gls{th}-T1 trigger requires a signal larger than $1.75\,I_{\text{VEM}}^{\text{peak}}$ in each of the three \gls{pmt}s.
This trigger is designed to select large signals that are not necessarily spread in time and is particularly sensitive to inclined showers with a stronger muonic component.

The \gls{tot} trigger requires that at least 13 bins within a sliding window of \SI{3}{\micro\s} exceed $0.2\,I_{\text{VEM}}^{\text{peak}}$ in coincidence in at least two \gls{pmt}s.
This trigger is designed to select sequences of smaller signals spread over time, as expected from the electromagnetic component of the shower.
It is therefore well suited to lower-energy showers and to events in which the detector is far from the shower core.

In 2014, two additional station-level trigger modes were introduced.
These triggers were designed to improve the sensitivity to the electromagnetic component of the shower and to extend the energy range over which the array can detect events.

The first of these triggers is the \gls{totd} trigger.
Traces with a significant electromagnetic component resemble a \gls{tot} signal, with a fast rise followed by an exponential decay.
After deconvolution, the expected signal becomes approximately flat, while preserving the fluctuations associated with particle counting.
The \gls{tot} requirement is then applied to the deconvolved trace.

The second additional trigger is the multiplicity-of-positive-steps trigger, \gls{mops}.
This trigger is independent of the \gls{vem} calibration and is based on the signal pattern expected from multiple low-amplitude particles arriving over an extended time interval.
It counts the number of positive steps within a \SI{3}{\micro\s} sliding window, where a positive step is defined as an increase in signal amplitude in consecutive bins.
To be counted, the step must be larger than the typical noise level and smaller than the amplitude expected from a vertical muon.
If more than four such positive-step instances are found, a \gls{mops} trigger is issued.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/chapter_data/provisory_t1t2t3.png}
    \caption{Summary of the T1, T2, and T3 trigger levels, their requirements, and their typical rates.
    The \gls{tot}, \gls{totd}, and \gls{mops} triggers are automatically promoted to the T2 level.}
    \label{fig:t1t2t3}
\end{figure}

The second trigger level, T2, is applied locally at each station to reduce the trigger rate to a value compatible with the bandwidth of the communication system.
All \gls{tot}, \gls{totd}, and \gls{mops} triggers are automatically promoted to T2.
By contrast, a \gls{th}-T1 trigger is promoted to T2 only if the signal exceeds a higher threshold of $3.2\,I_{\text{VEM}}^{\text{peak}}$ in coincidence in the three \gls{pmt}s.
At this stage, the trigger rate is reduced to about \SI{20}{\Hz} per station.

The T2 information, namely the trigger type and time stamp, is then sent to the \gls{cdas}, where array-level triggers are formed from spatial and temporal coincidences among stations.
This defines the third trigger level, T3, which initiates the data acquisition for the event.
Once a T3 is issued, the \gls{fadc} traces from all stations with a T2 trigger are sent to \gls{cdas}, together with those from stations that raised a T1, provided that their trigger time lies within \SI{30}{\micro\s} of the T3.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/chapter_data/provisory_t3.png}
    \caption{Examples of the two T3 triggering modes.
    Left: the three-fold configuration \textit{ToT2C1\&3C2}.
    Right: the four-fold configuration \textit{2C1\&3C2\&4C4}.
    Figure adapted from Fig.~2.14 of Ref.~\cite{Silli:2021Thesis}.}
    \label{fig:t3}
\end{figure}

The search for temporal and spatial groupings is done using two types of T3 algorithms.
Both of them look for T2-triggered stations in a compact configuration.
The compactness is described by the number of crowns over which the triggered stations are spread.
A crown is a way of counting hexagons outward from a central station.

The first configuration, \textit{ToT2C1\&3C2}, requires a coincidence of at least three nearby stations passing any combination of \gls{tot}, \gls{totd}, and \gls{mops} triggers.
These stations must be in a compact pattern, such that one station has another one in the first crown and a third one in the second crown. 
Since the \gls{tot} trigger has a very low background rate, this T3 mode has a low contamination and is highly efficient for showers with zenith angle below \SI{60}{\degree}.

The second T3 configuration, denoted \textit{2C1\&3C2\&4C4}, is more permissive.
It requires a four-fold coincidence of T2 stations, independently of their trigger type.
In this case, one triggered station must have another in the first crown, a third one in the second crown, and the fourth may be as far as the fourth crown.
This configuration is particularly relevant for more inclined showers, whose triggered stations can be spread over a wider area on the ground.

The T1, T2 and T3 selection are done online and the data is stored for later reconstruction where the next two trigger levels are applied.
The physical trigger, T4,is designed to reject accidental coincidences and retain physically originated showers.
For vertical showers,two T4 criteria are used.

The first T4 criterion, denoted \textit{3ToT}, originally required three nearby stations having a \gls{tot} trigger in a non-aligned pattern, but was later updated to also include combinations of the \gls{totd} and \gls{mops} triggers.
In addition, the trigger times of the three stations must be compatible with a plane shower front moving at the speed of light.

The second T4 criterion, denoted \textit{4C1}, requires four nearby triggered stations without imposing any condition on the trigger type.
As in the 3ToT case, their times must be compatible with a plane shower front moving at the speed of light.
This criterion complements the 3ToT selection and improves the efficiency for events that are not selected by the more restrictive \gls{tot}-based condition.

To ensure a reliable reconstruction of the event, the 6T5 fiducial quality trigger is applied.
It requires the station with the highest signal to be surrounded by six active neighbouring stations at the time of the event.
This condition ensures that the shower core is well contained within the array, improves the accuracy of the reconstructed quantities, and allows the effective area to coincide with the geometrical one for energies higher than full efficiency.

\section{Trigger Efficiency}
\label{sec:efficiency}

In order to calculate the exposure from the purely geometrical aperture of the array, it is necessary to establish an energy threshold above which the detector can be treated as fully efficient.
Below this threshold, the probability of detecting and reconstructing a shower depends on the primary energy and arrival direction, and these efficiency effects must be taken into account explicitly.
However, this effects are only taken into account for the unfolding correction to remove the detector influence on the spectrum.
For this reason, the definition of the dataset used in this work requires the identification of a threshold above which the detector can be treated as fully efficient. :contentReference[oaicite:0]{index=0}

The efficiency of the \sd{433} array was studied in detail in Ref.~\cite{GAP:Rodriguez:2024:efficiency}, using simulated showers and validating the results with measured data.
For this, CORSIKA simulations were used using EPOS-LHC as the high energy hadronic model and FLUKA for the lower energies.
Seven primary masses were simulated and reconstructed. 
Also, a combined mass result was estimated using the \gls{gsf} mix.
The study considers the probability that a shower with a given energy and zenith angle is successfully reconstructed by the array, after requiring the \mbox{6T5} condition and including the contribution of the \gls{totd} and \gls{mops} station triggers.
The final parameterization is given in the \sd{433} energy scale, after correcting the simulation-based efficiency using the attenuation and calibration chain adopted for data. :contentReference[oaicite:1]{index=1}

For events with zenith angles up to \SI{40}{\degree}, the efficiency is parameterized as
\[
\epsilon(E) = \Phi \left( a_0 + a_1 \lg\left(\frac{E}{10^{17}\,\mathrm{eV}}\right) \right),
\]
where \(\Phi\) is the cumulative distribution function of the standard normal distribution.
The fitted parameters for the \gls{gsf} mix are \(a_0 = 4.213 \pm 0.032\) and \(a_1 = 5.496 \pm 0.044\).
The corresponding efficiency estimators and fit are shown in \cref{fig:sd433_efficiency_curve} for the \gls{gsf} mix together with Proton and Iron as extreme cases. :contentReference[oaicite:2]{index=2}

    The inset highlights the region near full efficiency.

Two different data-driven validations were done for the SD-433.
However, it is important to note that both are not independent of the core and energy reconstruction procedure since both require reconstructed event observables and calibrated energies.
The assessment of the threshold in the data can only be performed once the energy reconstruction and calibration chain has been defined.
Nevertheless, these tests provide valuable data-driven validation of the threshold inferred from simulations.

To validate the simulation-based efficiency, Ref.~\cite{Rodriguez:2024Efficiency} tests the isotropy of the arrival directions in data above different energy thresholds.
Assuming isotropic arrival directions, the distribution in \(\sin^2\theta\) should be uniform up to the maximum zenith angle considered and quantified through an Anderson-Darling test.
The results show that the hypothesis of isotropic arrival becomes compatible with the data from \(\lg(E/\mathrm{eV}) = 16.8\), while \(\lg(E/\mathrm{eV}) = 16.7\) still shows measurable deviations from the full-efficiency expectation.
In this sense, adopting \(\lg(E/\mathrm{eV}) = 16.8\) as the first bin of the spectrum provides a conservative threshold above which the array can be treated as fully efficient. :contentReference[oaicite:3]{index=3}

As an additional cross-check, the spatial distribution of the data reconstructed core positions was studied.
This test was carried out using events reconstructed with zenith lower than\(\SI{40}{\degree}\).
If the detector is fully efficient and the selected events are isotropically distributed, the reconstructed cores are expected to populate the 6T5 area uniformly.
The position of the hottest station is subtracted to condense the array into a single hexagon.
After that, a 2D histogram of the core position is done, the bin size is chosen to be compatible with the resolution of the detector \cite{GAP:Gonzalez:2020:geometry}.
To conserve the symmetry of the array, hexagonal binning was chosen.
The outermost bins close to the 6T5 hexagon border are excluded since events reconstructed close to it can fall on either side of the boundary and introduce border effects, biasing the efficiency estimation. % TODO: refine wording once the final implementation details of the binning study are fixed.
\cref{fig:sd433_core_maps} shows the histogram of the difference from the average number of reconstructed cores , weighted by the dispersion to highlight deviations,
\begin{align}
    \Delta n_{i} = \frac{n_i-n_{avg}}{\sigma_n}.
\end{align}

When no energy cut is applied, the artefacts present in the triangles formed by the central and the surrounding stations occur due to the reconstructed core being pulled to this region in low energy events, below full efficiency.
As energy increases, these artefacts start to fade out as the core position becomes more uniform.
However, at \(\lg(E/\mathrm{eV}) = 16.7\), the core map still shows an overdensity of events around the central station.
This is consistent with a situation in which the reconstruction tends to pull the core position toward the hottest station when the array has not yet reached full efficiency.
From \(\lg(E/\mathrm{eV}) = 16.8\) onwards, this feature is no longer apparent, and the core distribution becomes compatible with a uniform population of the fiducial area.

    \caption{Distribution of reconstructed core positions in the fiducial area for different minimum-energy thresholds, using events with \(\theta < \SI{40}{\degree}\).
    The outermost bins are excluded to avoid edge effects associated with events reconstructed close to the fiducial boundary.

To quantify this effect, the observed core distributions were compared with a uniform expectation by means of the Poisson deviance,
\begin{align}
D^2 = 2 \sum_i \left[ \nu - n_i + n_i \ln\left(\frac{n_i}{\nu}\right) \right],
\label{Eq:Poisson_dev}
\end{align}

where \(n_i\) is the number of events in bin \(i\), and \(\nu\) is the expected number for a uniform distribution.
The resulting deviance as a function of the minimum energy threshold is shown in \cref{fig:sd433_core_deviance}.
The deviance decreases with increasing threshold and falls below unity at \(\lg(E_{\min}/\mathrm{eV}) = 16.8\), again supporting the adoption of this value as the first bin in the full-efficiency regime. 

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\linewidth]{figures/chapter_data/core_distribution_deviance.png}
    \caption{Poisson deviance of the reconstructed core distribution with respect to a uniform expectation, as a function of the minimum-energy threshold, for events with \(\theta < \SI{40}{\degree}\).
    The deviance falls below unity at \(\lg(E_{\min}/\mathrm{eV}) = 16.8\), supporting the adoption of this value as the beginning of the full-efficiency regime.}
    \label{fig:sd433_core_deviance}
\end{figure}

For this reason, in the following, \(\lg(E/\mathrm{eV}) = 16.8\) is adopted as the full-efficiency threshold for the \sd{433} dataset.
Above this energy, the exposure can be calculated from the purely geometric aperture of the array.

\section{Array stability}

In 2014, the \sd{433} started as a single hexagon around the Tina Turner.
Later, between 2017 and 2019, 12 more stations were gradually included to form the current 7-hexagon configuration, as it is shown in \cref{fig:array}.
Two of the stations of the first hexagon, Chichino Jr. and Guili Jr. were relocated at the end of 2017 to get them to a position closer to the designed one.
In 2018, it was discovered by Roncoroni et al.~\cite{GAP:Roncoroni:2021:recovery} that the non-triggered stations from the \sd{433} were incorrectly flagged during the CDAS merge and the 6T5 condition could not be properly evaluated leading to a very low number of 6T5 events.
After the replacement of the \sd{750} radios, it was possible to include the non-triggered stations via the \textit{fast CDAS} trigger algorithm, normalizing the acquisition after July 1st. 2018.
In the top panel of \cref{fig:event_rate} the daily number of events is shown after the fast CDAS algorithm was deployed.
\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/chapter_data/event_rate.pdf}
    \caption{Daily 6T5 event rate for the \sd{433} from the inclusion of the fast CDAS algorithm up to the end of Phase I when the electronics upgrade started. In the bottom plot, the event rate is normalized by the number of active hexagons calculated from the T2life file. The average event rate is also shown.}
    \label{fig:event_rate}
\end{figure}
When normalizing by the number of active hexagons an average of 6.77 events per day per hexagon that fulfil the 6T5 condition are expected with a $\sigma=1.84$.
Even though the duty cycle of the surface detector is ~100\%, some periods of bad data acquisition are expected.
These periods are found evaluating the time between successive events.
Assuming that the number of expected events follow a Poisson distribution, the probability of observing a time interval $t>T$,
\begin{align}
    P(t>T)=e^{-FA\,T},
\end{align}
is given by the expected number of events per unit of time and area, $F$, the affective area of active hexagons between the two events.
An unstable period can be declared when the probability $P(T)$ is lower than a threshold, $\alpha$, calculated from deviations of more than $2\sigma$ of an exponential model, obtaining $\alpha=10^{-2.5}$\cite{GAP:Gonzalez:2022:exposure}.
The periods with a $P(T)<\alpha$, denominated \textit{bad periods}, are shaded in figure \cref{fig:event_rate}.
The total acquisition time lost in during the bad periods correspond to 169.6 weeks of the total 1269, around $13.37\%$.

\section{Exposure of the \sd{433}}
With the results from previous sections, the question of the exposure can finally be tackled.
When the detector is in the full efficiency regime, the acceptance of the detector becomes independent of the energy and mass of the particle and the zenith angle.
In this way, the aperture of the detector can be calculated as the effective area of the detector integrated over solid angle.
Given the 6T5 condition, the effective area of the hexagons can be calculated as the surface where the station with the maximum signal is the closest one.
This is analogous to the Weigner-Seitz cell used in Solid State Physics.
For a triangular array, this corresponds to a hexagon with radius $r=\sqrt{d/2}$, where $d=\SI{433}{m}$ is the distance between detectors. 
The effective aperture per hexagon is then
\begin{align}
    A_\text{6T5}=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}d^2\pi(\sin^2\theta_\text{max}-\sin^2\theta_\text{min}) =\SI{0.255}{\km\squared\sr\yr}. 
\end{align}

The effective number number of active hexagons, $N_\text{hex}$, is calculated for every minute from the T2life files.
With this, the differential exposure then becomes trivial, 
\begin{align}
    \epsilon=N_\text{hex}A_{\text{6T5}},
\end{align}
and the total exposure is the integral over time.

The accumulated exposure of each individual hexagon is shown in \cref{fig:exposure} where hexagons are designated with the name of the central station.
It can be seen that the hexagons centered around Guili Jr., Pipi Jr., and Catherina were deployed in May 2019, completing the current seven array configuration.

The total accumulated exposure for zenith angle $\leq40^\circ$, shown in black, is \totalExposure after the removal of bad periods. With this, the raw spectrum in each energy bin,
\begin{align}
    J_\text{raw}(E)=\frac{N}{\varepsilon\Delta E},
\end{align}
can be determined. Where $N$ denotes the number of events and $\Delta E$ is the energy width of the bin.

\label{sec:stability}
