\chapter{Energy reconstruction}
\label{chap:energy_reconstruction}
\label{chapter:reconstruction}

This chapter describes the reconstruction chain that connects the station-level measurements of an event to an estimate of the primary cosmic-ray energy.
In \cref{sec:reconstruction_shower_size}, the \Offline reconstruction is used to determine the shower geometry and to estimate the signal at a reference distance from the shower axis through a fit of the \gls{ldf}.
The zenith-angle dependence caused by atmospheric attenuation is then determined and removed in \cref{sec:cic}.
Finally, in \cref{sec:calibration}, the corrected shower size is calibrated against the energy estimator of the \sd{750} to assign an energy to each \sd{433} event.

\section{Reconstruction of the shower size}
\label{sec:reconstruction_shower_size}

The shower reconstruction proceeds in two stages.
First, the relative start times of the signals measured by the participating stations are used to determine the arrival direction of the shower and the impact position of its core on the ground.
The recorded signals are then fitted as a function of the perpendicular distance to the shower axis using an empirical \gls{ldf}.
The value of this function at the reference distance of \SI{300}{\m}, denoted by $S(300)$, provides the ground-level shower-size estimator used for the \sd{433}.
Because the core position affects the station distances entering the \gls{ldf}, the geometry and shower size are finally determined in a combined fit initialized with the results of the separate reconstructions.
A detailed description of the event-reconstruction procedure used for the surface detector can be found in Ref.~\cite{PierreAuger:2020:SDReconstruction}.
Here, the main elements relevant to the reconstruction of the \sd{433} events are summarized, with particular emphasis on the steps implemented for the present analysis.

\subsection{Geometrical reconstruction}
\label{subsec:geometry_ldf}

The geometrical reconstruction uses the positions and signal start times of the detectors participating in the event.
Its purpose is to determine the position of the shower core on the ground, $\vec{x}_{\mathrm{c}}$, and the unit vector along the shower axis, $\hat{a}$, whose components are the direction cosines from which the zenith angle, $\theta$, and the azimuth angle, $\phi$, are obtained.

The reconstruction begins with a plane-front fit to the station start times, which provides an initial estimate of the arrival direction.
The signal-weighted barycentre of the participating stations is used as the initial estimate of the core position.
These estimates are subsequently refined using a curved description of the shower front.

In the \Offline reconstruction, the most advanced particles of the shower, which define the extreme shower front, are approximated by a sphere expanding at the speed of light from a virtual origin $\vec{x}_0$ at time $t_0$.
The predicted time at which this front reaches a position $\vec{x}$ is
\begin{align}
    c\,t_\mathrm{sh}(\vec{x}) = c\,t_0 + \lvert \vec{x}-\vec{x}_0 \rvert .
    \label{eq:spherical_shower_front}
\end{align}
The fit minimizes the squared differences between the measured and predicted start times,
\begin{align}
    \chi^2 = \sum_i \frac{\left[t_i-t_\mathrm{sh}(\vec{x}_i)\right]^2}{\sigma_{t_i}^2}.
    \label{eq:geometry_chi2}
\end{align}
where $t_i$ and $\sigma_{t_i}$ are the start time measured by station $i$ and its uncertainty, respectively.

The core position is refined together with the lateral distribution of the station signals.
Once $\vec{x}_{\mathrm{c}}$ is determined, the reconstructed shower direction is given by
\begin{align}
    \hat{a}
    =
    \frac{\vec{x}_{0}-\vec{x}_{\mathrm{c}}}
    {\left|\vec{x}_{0}-\vec{x}_{\mathrm{c}}\right|}.
    \label{eq:shower_axis}
\end{align}
The radius of curvature at the core position is
\begin{align}
    R_{0}=\left|\vec{x}_{0}-\vec{x}_{\mathrm{c}}\right|.
\end{align}

For events with at least five participating stations, the radius of curvature is determined from the timing fit.
For events with lower station multiplicity, the number of degrees of freedom is insufficient to constrain the curvature, and $R_{0}$ is fixed according to a parametrization obtained from higher-multiplicity events.

The left panel of \cref{fig:sd433_event_footprint_ldf} illustrates the geometrical reconstruction of an \sd{433} event.
The marker size is proportional to the station signal, while the colour scale represents its start time, progressing from earlier signals in blue to later signals in green.
The black line indicates the reconstructed arrival direction projected onto the ground, and the cross marks the reconstructed core position.
\subsection{Lateral-distribution function}
\label{subsec:ldf}

The shower size is then estimated by modeling the fall-off of the signal $S(r)$ with the distance $r$ to the shower axis using a \gls{ldf} ~\cite{Silli:2021:PerformanceSD433}.
Intrinsic shower-to-shower fluctuations produce variations in the longitudinal development, such that individual events may be sampled at different stages of their development.
As a consequence, the \gls{ldf} exhibits variations on an event-by-event basis, preventing the reconstruction of all the parameters in the shower. 
Instead, the reconstruction is based on an \textit{average} LDF, $\langle S(r)\rangle$, and the expected signal at a fixed reference distance, $r_\text{opt}$, is adopted as the shower size.
The optimal distance of \qty{300}{m} for the \sd{433} is determined by fitting the average LDF and identifying the distance at which the reconstructed shower size is minimally dependent on the LDF slope, following the method described in Ref.~\cite{Newton:2007:OptimalDistance}.
The dependence of the optimal distance on the assumed LDF shape, the primary energy, and the zenith angle remains at approximately the 5\% level~\cite{PierreAuger:2020:SDReconstruction}.
The average LDF is described using an empirically-chosen model based on a modified \gls{nkg}function,
\begin{align}
  \langle S(r)\rangle =
    S(r_\text{opt})
    \left(\frac{r}{r_\text{opt}}\right)^\beta
    \left(\frac{r + r_\text{scale}}{r_\text{opt} + r_\text{scale}}\right)^\beta,
\end{align}
%
where $\beta$ is the LDF slope and $r_\text{scale}=700\,\text{m}$ is a scale parameter.

The slope of the \gls{ldf} was determined on an event-by-event basis using a set of high-quality events with at least five triggered stations, for which $\beta$ could be left as a free parameter in the fit~\cite{Silli:2021:PerformanceSD433}.
The fitted values were then used to parametrize the average dependence of the slope on the shower size and zenith angle as
\begin{align}
    \beta(\theta,y)=a(\theta)+b(\theta)y
\end{align}
where $y=\lg[S(300)/\text{VEM}]$.
The coefficients that govern the dependence in zenith angle, $a(\theta)$ and $b(\theta)$ are modeled as second degree polynomials in $\sec\theta$,
\begin{align}
    \begin{pmatrix}
        a \\
        b
    \end{pmatrix}
    =
    \begin{pmatrix}
        -3.72 & 1.30  & 0.055 \\
         0.98 & -1.30 & 0.385
    \end{pmatrix}
    \begin{pmatrix}
        1 \\
        \sec\theta \\
        \sec^{2}\theta
    \end{pmatrix}.
    \label{eq:beta_parameterization}
\end{align}
The parametrization describes the event-by-event fitted slopes with an average relative difference of approximately $2\%$ over the considered range of shower sizes and zenith angles~\cite{Silli:2021:PerformanceSD433}.
Once determined, this parametrization was fixed in the reconstruction of the complete data set, allowing events with insufficient station multiplicity to constrain the LDF slope to be reconstructed with the average LDF shape.
With the LDF shape fixed by the parametrization of $\beta$, the shower size $S(300)$ and the core position $\vec{x}_{\mathrm{c}}$ are determined simultaneously through a maximum-likelihood fit.
The likelihood accounts for the measured signals in the triggered stations and for active stations that did not trigger.
Although no signal trace is available for these non-triggered stations, their presence constrains the fit, since an LDF predicting a signal above the trigger threshold at their positions is disfavoured.
In this way, the LDF fit determines $S(300)$ and refines the core position obtained from the initial geometrical reconstruction.

\section{Correction for zenith-angle attenuation}
\label{sec:cic}

The reconstructed signal $S(300)$ provides an estimator of the shower size at ground level and is therefore used as a proxy of the energy of the primary cosmic ray.
However, for a fixed primary energy, its value depends on the zenith angle because the atmospheric attenuation due traversing a larger atmospheric slant-depth before reaching the detector.
One of the sources of this attenuation is the energy loss of the particles due to the atmospheric interaction and the more inclined showers are generally later developed than the vertical showers.
Also, since showers possess a muonic and electromagnetic components, these two attenuate in different way due to the higher penetration of muons.
In this way, the electromagnetic to muonic ratio of the component changes with the zenith angle.
Finally, there is a component given of the interaction of the particles inside the detector. 
One one hand, more inclined particles traverse a longer path inside the detector allowing for the particles to deposit more energy inside the tank.
This effect is specially noticeable for the electrons and photons of the electromagnetic component of the shower, since the muons cross completely the detector.
Consequently, $S(300)$ must be corrected for atmospheric attenuation before it can be used as an energy estimator.
This correction was first studied by Hirsil et al in ~\cite{Hirsil:1961:CIC} in the 1961 and is determined from data using the constant-intensity-cut method described below, using a purely phenomenological method so that is independent of Hadronic models and primary mass composition assumptions.
The shower size is corrected using as a zenith angle independent estimator the $S(300)$ expected for a shower with the same energy at reference angle $\theta_{\text{ref}}=30^\circ$.
This angle is chosen near the median of the zenith angle range used, $0-45^\circ$, for the\sd{433} dividing the number of events roughly in half.
In this way, the size of the correction and its uncertainty is reduced.
The corrected shower size, $S_{30}$ is obtained via the attenuation function,
\begin{align}
    S(300)=\frac{S_{30}}{f(\theta,S_{\text{30}})}
    \label{eq:attenuation}
\end{align}
which is chosen by construction so that $f(300,30^\circ)=1$.

\subsection{The Constant-intensity cut method}
\label{subsec:cic_method}
To determine the attenuation function, the \gls{cic} method is used.
Since at energies below $8\,\text{EeV}$, the cosmic ray flux can be treated as isotropic in arrival direction.
Because of the solid-angle projection, equal exposure can be obtained when binning in $\sin^2\theta$.
Above full efficiency, equal number of events are expected in these intervals for a fixed energy threshold.
For the \sd{433}, nine zenith angle bins are used.
Within each zenith angle bin the events are ranked in decreasing $S(300)$. 
Given the isotropic distribution, selecting the same intensity(i.e., $N$) in each zenith angle, events of the same rank are expected to have the same $S(300)$. 
Through this, the $f(S_{30},\theta)$ can then be estimated for different intensities since a one to one correspondence cannot be performed.
To be statistically significant, intensities $[100,200,500,1000,2000 and 5000]$ were used.
The statistical uncertainty assigned to the shower size at rank $N$ is estimated using the neighboring statistics,
\begin{align}
    \sigma_{S(300)_N}=\frac{S(300)_{N-\sqrt{N}}-S(300)_{N+\sqrt{N}}}{2}
\end{align}
The fit is performed using $x=\sin^2\theta-\sin^2\theta_{\text{ref}}$ and $y=\lg(S_{30}/S_0)$ as independent variables, where the scale factor $S_0=40\,\text{VEM}$ is used to reduce the correlation in the parameters.
The dependence of the attenuation function in these parameters is expressed as a third degree polynomial in $x$,
\begin{align}
    f_{\text{CIC}}(\theta,S_{30})=1+a_1(y)x+a_2(y)x^2+cx^3
\end{align}
where the coefficients $a_1$, $a_2$ are first degree polinomials in $y$,
\begin{align}
    a_i(y)=b_1+b_2y,
\end{align}
and $c$ is a constant factor.
On a first step, the different intensities are fitted sepparately.
However, finally a global fit using the individually fitted parameters is performed
The obtained give a $\chi^2/N_{\text{dof}=31.79/43}$ and best fit parameters are presented in \cref{table:cic_parameters}.
Given the explicit dependence of $f_{\text{CIC}}$ in $S_{30}$ an inverse fit has to be applied.
In \cref{fig:cic_global_fit} the $S_{300}$ for the different intensities is shown together with the fitted attenuation function.
In the inset below, the different attenuation curves are shown together to see the explicit dependence in $S_{30}$.
However this dependence is minor and is below $4\%$ on the correction factor, so the $S_{30}$ difference is within $1\%$.
