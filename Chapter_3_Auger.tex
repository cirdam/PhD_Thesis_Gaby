\chapter{The Pierre Auger Observatory}
\label{chapter:auger}

The Pierre Auger Observatory was designed to study different observables of \gls{uhecr}, including the energy spectrum, mass composition and arrival directions, based on a hybrid detection technique.
Subsequent extensions increased the energy range towards lower energies, while more recent upgrades introduced additional detection techniques, leading to a multi-hybrid era of cosmic-ray detection.

Located in the Pampa Amarilla, in the Malargüe and San Rafael departments of Mendoza, Argentina, the Pierre Auger Observatory is the largest cosmic-ray observatory in the world by instrumented surface area.
It comprises more than 1600 surface detector stations that span a surface of $3000\mathrm{\,km^2}$ and twenty-four standard \gls{fd} telescopes overlooking the array from four sites: Los Leones, Los Morados, Loma Amarilla and Coihueco~\cite{PierreAuger:2015:Observatory}.
Three additional \gls{heat} telescopes near Coihueco extend the fluorescence observations towards lower energies.
While the \gls{fd} telescopes measure the fluorescence light generated by \gls{eas} on clear, moonless nights, the \gls{sd} detects the particles generated by the shower that reach the ground.
The Pampa Amarilla is a plateau situated at an average height of 1400\,m above sea level, corresponding to a vertical atmospheric depth of about \SI{875}{\g\per\cm\squared}~\cite{PierreAuger:2015:Observatory}.
A map of the observatory is shown in \cref{fig:map_detector} together with a picture of one of the \gls{sd} detectors and the \gls{fd} site in the background.
Each circle in the map represents a surface detector station, where three triangular arrays are formed.
Originally, the detectors were spaced by 1500\,m and then two nested arrays were deployed spaced 750\,m and 433\,m to extend the energy range to lower energies.

The hybrid detection technique combines the quasi-calorimetric measurement of the cosmic-ray primary energy with the sampling of shower particles at ground level.
The fluorescence light is produced by nitrogen molecules in the atmosphere excited by charged shower particles, tracing the longitudinal development and the energy deposited in the atmosphere.
The primary energy is obtained by adding an invisible-energy correction to this calorimetric energy, accounting mainly for the energy carried by neutrinos and muons that is not deposited in the atmosphere~\cite{PierreAuger:2015:Observatory}.
The surface detector samples the secondary particles originating in the shower when they reach the ground.
The \gls{sd} operates with a duty cycle of $\sim100\%$, compared to the $\sim15\%$ duty cycle of the \gls{fd}, which is limited by night-time and atmospheric observing conditions~\cite{PierreAuger:2015:Observatory}.
During periods when both systems operate, some showers are sampled simultaneously by both detectors, providing hybrid observations.
Combining the timing information from the \gls{sd} stations and \gls{fd} telescopes improves the reconstruction of the shower geometry.
Suitable showers reconstructed independently by both detectors are then used to calibrate the \gls{sd} shower-size estimator against the primary energy measured by the \gls{fd}.
Using this hybrid calibration allows the energy scale to be transferred to the much larger dataset of \gls{sd} events, including those without a simultaneous \gls{fd} observation.

The densest of the \gls{sd} arrays, the \sd{433}, which is the subject of this thesis, requires an indirect energy calibration in the analysis presented here.
This calibration is performed using events detected in coincidence with the 750\,m array, the \sd{750}, whose energy scale is calibrated with the \gls{fd}~\cite{PierreAuger:2021:InfillSpectrum}.
The \sd{433} provides coverage below the second-knee region, and its calibration through the \sd{750} allows the spectral feature to be characterized on a common energy scale inherited from the \gls{fd}.

\section{The surface detector}
\label{sec:auger_surface_detector}

The \gls{sd} samples the particles originated in the \gls{eas} when reaching the ground using \gls{wcd}s.
When charged particles traverse the water inside a \gls{wcd}, they emit Cherenkov light, which is collected by the three \gls{pmt}s overlooking the tank.
Both the muonic and electromagnetic component of the shower produce the light inside the water.
Each of the \gls{wcd}s consists of a cylindrical polyethylene tank of 3.6\,m in diameter and 1.55\,m height, giving a surface of $\SI{10}{\m\squared}$.
Inside each tank, a Tyvek reflective liner contains $~\SI{12000}{\liter}$ of hyper pure water with three windows where three large \gls{pmt}s overlook the water.
In \cref{fig:tank} a schematic of the vertical cutout of a \gls{wcd} is shown together with a picture describing the outer elements.
Each tank has its own self-powered electronics for the station-level trigger and performance diagnostic autonomously.
Because of this, a solar panel and a battery are used to supply power in the field.
Communication with \gls{cdas}, in the city of Malargüe, is carried out using a radio connected to the nearest \gls{fd} site and the time is obtained with a GPS beacon.

The \gls{pmt} signal is digitized as a time trace and the different trigger algorithms use the structure and integrated signal to detect where particles from a \gls{eas} are traversing the detector.
When time and space correlated signals of three or more detectors can be established, an event level trigger can be issued and the signals and status of relevant signals are acquired.
However, a common calibration unit is needed to compare the signal of the different \gls{wcd}s.
Because of this, the atmospheric muons that constantly traverse the detector provide a useful and ever-present calibration reference.
The \gls{vem} is defined as the average integrated charge, $Q_{\mathrm{VEM}}$, produced by a muon that crosses the detector vertically through its center.

An example of the charge and pulse-height histograms used for the calibration is shown in \cref{fig:sd_muon_calibration}.
The black histograms show the background sample and the red dashed histograms show the vertical, central muons selected by the external telescope.
The muon peaks in the background histograms define $Q_{\mathrm{VEM}}^{\mathrm{peak}}$ and $I_{\mathrm{VEM}}^{\mathrm{peak}}$, respectively.
The charge provides a reference for integrated shower signals in VEM, while the pulse height provides a reference for setting uniform signal thresholds.

The background sample includes muons with different directions and crossing through different parts of the tanks, so the peak of the charge-histogram does not coincide exactly with $Q_{\mathrm{VEM}}$.
However, this is accounted for through previous measurements done in the laboratory using an external muon telescope that selected vertical, central muons.
For individual PMTs, $Q_{\mathrm{VEM}}^{\mathrm{peak}}/Q_{\mathrm{VEM}} = 1.03 \pm 0.02$, with the difference arising from the distribution of track lengths, photoelectron statistics, and light-collection effects~\cite{Bertou:2006:Calibration}.
These calibration quantities are regularly tracked to find changes in the detector response to maintain a common signal scale between stations.

However, this thesis uses data recorded with the original electronics.

\section{The three surface-detector arrays}
\label{sec:auger_surface_arrays}

The three arrays of the surface detector use the same \gls{wcd}s, with stations arranged on triangular grids of different spacing.
At lower primary energies, the particle density at ground decreases, and a denser array is needed to sample a shower.
The denser arrays extend the measurement towards lower energies, where the larger cosmic-ray flux allows sufficient statistics to be collected over a smaller area.

The \sd{1500} was the first array of the Observatory, covering approximately \SI{3000}{\km\squared} with about 1600 detectors.
The large covered area allows the measurement of the low cosmic-ray flux at the highest energies, including the ankle and the suppression of the spectrum~\cite{PierreAuger:2020:Spectrum,PierreAuger:2025:Declination}.
For showers with zenith angles below $60^\circ$, the array is fully efficient above \SI{2.5}{\exa\eV}~\cite{PierreAuger:2020:Spectrum}.

The \sd{750} was deployed in the north-western part of the \sd{1500}, near the Coihueco \gls{fd} site and the \gls{heat} telescopes, to extend the observations to lower energies.
Its 71 stations cover approximately \SI{27}{\km\squared}~\cite{Silli:2021:Thesis}.
The published \sd{750} spectrum extends down to \SI{100}{\peta\eV} for zenith angles below $40^\circ$.
Its proximity to the fluorescence telescopes also allows hybrid detection of lower-energy showers, which are used for the energy calibration~\cite{PierreAuger:2021:InfillSpectrum}.

The \sd{433}, the subject of this thesis, is the densest array, with 19 stations nested within the \sd{750}.
It started as a single hexagon around the Tina Turner station, and the seven-hexagon configuration shown in \cref{fig:sd433_array} was completed in May 2019~\cite{Silli:2021:PerformanceSD433}.
It extends the spectral measurement below the second knee, allowing this feature to be studied on both sides in combination with the \sd{750}.
The deployment, event selection, trigger efficiency and exposure of the \sd{433} are described in \cref{chap:sd433_dataset}.

\section{The fluorescence detector}
\label{sec:auger_fd}

Complementary to the \gls{sd}, the fluorescence detector provides a quasi-calorimetric estimate of the energy of primary cosmic rays during clear, moonless nights.
These observing conditions limit its duty cycle to approximately $15\%$.
Each \gls{fd} site consists of six telescopes overlooking the \gls{sd}, providing $180^\circ$ of horizontal coverage.
Each telescope has a $30^\circ\times30^\circ$ field of view and uses Schmidt optics, as shown in \cref{fig:fd_telescope_schematic}.
Light enters through a 2.2\,m diameter aperture, passing through a UV filter to reduce background light.
A glass corrector ring at the window reduces optical aberrations, and the spherical mirror focuses the light onto the camera.

The camera consists of 440 \gls{pmt}s arranged in a $22\times20$ array.
Each \gls{pmt} covers approximately $1.5^\circ$ of the sky.
As the shower crosses the field of view, the fluorescence light produces a track across the camera.
The signals collected by the pixels are used to estimate the light intensity and timing along the track~\cite{PierreAuger:2015:Observatory}.

Several calibrations are performed over time to ensure stable operation of the detector.
Absolute calibrations have used a drum source and, more recently, a moving point source scanned across the aperture~\cite{Schaeffer:2023:XYScanner}.
Relative calibrations use internal light sources to monitor changes in the response.

The shower geometry is determined from the observed track and the arrival times of the light.
The deposited energy is then measured as a function of atmospheric depth.
This requires accounting for atmospheric attenuation and contributions from direct and scattered Cherenkov light.
Integrating the energy-deposit profile gives a calorimetric estimate of the energy, while the position of its maximum, $\Xmax$, provides information about the primary mass.
A correction for invisible energy is also included when estimating the primary energy~\cite{PierreAuger:2015:Observatory}.

Three \gls{heat} telescopes were added later near the Coihueco site to lower the energy threshold of the \gls{fd}.
These telescopes use the same optical system and cameras as the standard \gls{fd} telescopes, but can be tilted upward by approximately $30^\circ$.
This allows them to detect lower-energy showers, which produce less fluorescence light and must therefore be observed from closer distances.
Their shower maximum can appear above the field of view of the standard \gls{fd} telescopes.
In the upward mode, \gls{heat} covers elevations of approximately $30^\circ$--$60^\circ$.
In the downward mode, its field of view overlaps with that of the Coihueco telescopes, allowing cross-calibration.
This extends observations of showers detected in common with the \sd{750} towards lower energies~\cite{PierreAuger:2015:Observatory}.
However, too few suitable events are observed in common with the \sd{433} to perform a direct energy calibration of this array with the fluorescence telescopes~\cite{Silli:2021:Thesis}.

\section{Reconstruction software}
\label{sec:auger_reconstruction_software}

There are two different implementations of the \gls{sd} event reconstruction, Observer within the \Offline framework and Herald, each used for different datasets based on their degree of development and functionality for each detector type~\cite{PierreAuger:2020:SDReconstruction}.
The \Offline framework supports both simulation and reconstruction, while Herald is associated with the central data acquisition system.
In this work, \Offline is used for the \sd{433} events, while the \sd{750} events reconstructed using Herald for the spectrum published in Ref.~\cite{PierreAuger:2021:InfillSpectrum} are reused.
